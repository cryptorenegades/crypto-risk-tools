How to use the calculator

  1. Choose your risk per trade first, before you look at the chart, and keep it fixed. Many disciplined traders use 0.5% to 2% of the account.
  2. Set the stop where the trade idea is proven wrong, not where the loss feels comfortable.
  3. Read the position size. That is the position value to enter. The line under it shows how many units (coins) that buys at your entry.
  4. If you use leverage, check the liquidation estimate. Your stop must sit well before it. The calculator warns you when it does not.

How the position size formula works

Position sizing starts from the loss, not the profit. You decide the dollars you accept losing if the trade is wrong, find the price where the idea fails, and let the distance between entry and stop decide how large the position can be.

  1. Amount at risk = account size × risk per trade %
  2. Units = amount at risk ÷ (entry - stop) for a long, ÷ (stop - entry) for a short
  3. Position size = units × entry
  4. Margin = position size ÷ leverage
  5. Estimated liquidation = entry × (1 - 1 ÷ leverage) for a long, entry × (1 + 1 ÷ leverage) for a short

Leverage only appears in the last two lines. It changes the margin you post and how close the liquidation price sits to your entry. It does not change the units or the dollars lost at the stop, as long as the stop fills before liquidation.

Worked example

These are the numbers loaded in the calculator above.

Long, $50,000 account, 1% risk, 5x leverage
  1. Amount at risk: 50,000 × 1% = $500.
  2. Entry $60,000, stop $57,000: $3,000 per unit, which is 5.00% below the entry.
  3. Units: 500 ÷ 3,000 = 0.1667. Position size: 0.1667 × 60,000 = $10,000, or 20% of the account.
  4. Margin at 5x: 10,000 ÷ 5 = $2,000.
  5. Estimated liquidation: 60,000 × (1 - 1 ÷ 5) = $48,000, 20% below the entry. The stop at $57,000 closes the position long before that.
  6. If the stop fills, the loss is 0.1667 × 3,000 = $500 plus fees and any slippage, at 1x or at 10x.

Short version. A $10,000 account at 2% risks $200. Entry $2,000 with a stop at $2,100 is $100 per unit, so the position is 2 units, worth $4,000. Switch the calculator to Short to check it.

The liquidation estimate and its limits

The estimate uses the common approximation entry × (1 - 1 ÷ leverage) for a long and entry × (1 + 1 ÷ leverage) for a short. Real platforms add maintenance margin and fees, so the real liquidation level arrives a little sooner than this figure. Always check the liquidation price your platform shows before you enter.

The calculator raises three flags:

  • Stop beyond liquidation. The platform would close the position before your stop does, so the loss is no longer the one you planned.
  • Liquidation within 1.5 percentage points of the stop. A fast market can skip past a stop. Little room between the two means slippage could reach liquidation.
  • Margin larger than the account. The stop is too close for the risk you chose at this leverage. Widen the stop and the position shrinks.

Common position sizing mistakes

  • Choosing leverage first.Picking 20x and then looking for a stop gets the order backwards. Size from the stop; leverage only decides the margin.
  • Moving the stop to fit a bigger position.The stop belongs where the idea fails. If the position that fits is too small to be worth it, the trade is the problem, not the stop.
  • Typing the margin into the size box.Many order forms ask for the position size or the units, not the margin. At 5x, entering $2,000 when the plan says $10,000 makes the position five times too small, and the reverse mistake makes it five times too big.
  • Raising risk after a loss.Bigger positions to win it back turn a normal losing streak into a deep drawdown. See how fast that compounds in the drawdown recovery calculator.
  • Forgetting fees and slippage.A stop is an order to exit, not a fixed exit price. Real losses run slightly above the planned amount, so many traders round the position size down.
