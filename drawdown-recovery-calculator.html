Why losses compound against you

A gain or a loss is a percentage of whatever the account holds at the time. After a loss the base is smaller, so the same percentage gain adds back fewer dollars than the loss took away.

Lose 20% of $10,000 and you have $8,000. A 20% gain on $8,000 is only $1,600, which leaves you at $9,600. Getting back to $10,000 takes $2,000, and $2,000 is 25% of $8,000. The deeper the loss, the wider that gap: 10% needs 11.11%, 30% needs 42.86%, 50% needs 100% and 75% needs 300%.

Why capping risk per trade matters

You cannot control whether the next trade works. You can control what one loss costs. That single decision sets how deep a normal losing streak can take the account.

  • At 1% risk per trade, ten losses in a row leave the account down 9.56%, which needs 10.57% to recover.
  • At 5% risk per trade, the same ten losses leave it down 40.13%, which needs 67.02% to recover.

Same streak, very different hole. The risk per trade table shows how many losses in a row each risk level can absorb before a 20% drawdown.

Worked example

$10,000 account, 30% drawdown
  1. The account peaks at $10,000 and falls 30% to $7,000.
  2. Getting back to $10,000 takes $3,000.
  3. $3,000 ÷ $7,000 = 0.4286, so the gain needed is 42.86%.
  4. Same answer from the formula: 1 ÷ (1 - 0.30) - 1 = 1 ÷ 0.70 - 1 = 0.4286.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a 30% gain undoes a 30% loss.It leaves you 9% short: $10,000 × 0.70 × 1.30 = $9,100.
  • Measuring from the deposit instead of the peak.Drawdown runs from the highest balance to the lowest point after it. Measuring from the deposit hides losses on gains you had already made.
  • Raising risk to recover faster.Bigger positions after a loss make the next loss bigger too, which deepens the hole the table describes.
  • Letting deposits hide a drawdown.Adding money to the account makes the balance look recovered. Track the drawdown on the capital you traded, not on what you added later.
